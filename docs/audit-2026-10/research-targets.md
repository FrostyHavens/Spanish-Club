# Realistic Spanish targets for a child who starts at 7: 15 minutes a day for 3 years (~225–275 h)

Research summary for the "Club de Español" end-goal question. WebFetch could not reach any domain (DNS failures), so every figure below comes from search-result abstracts and snippets, not from reading the full papers. Figures I could not verify are marked **[unverified]**. Each claim has a confidence level, and adult or teen evidence is labelled **[ADULT]**, **[TEEN]** or **[CHILD]**.

---

## 0. Bottom line

- **Time budget.** 15 min × 365 days is about 91 h a year. If she plays 80–90% of days, that is **73–82 h a year and about 220–275 h over 3 years**.
  - A "good" elementary school program (FLES: Foreign Language in the Elementary School), at ACTFL's minimum of 90 min a week, gives about **54 h a year**.
  - ACTFL's higher K-12 standard (30–40 min, 3–5 days a week) gives about **45–130 h a year**.
  - So in raw hours the game is roughly **a strong daily school program**. It has more retrieval per minute and no summer gap, but no teacher and almost no real conversation.
- **Honest end state at age 10** (after ~250 h):
  - **ACTFL Novice High overall.** That is CEFR **A1 solid**, with **A2 in listening and reading in places** (about the Cambridge *A1 Movers → A2 Flyers* band).
  - **Receptive vocabulary:** about **1,200–1,500 lemmas** (stretch 1,800).
  - **Productive vocabulary:** about **600–900**, plus **60–80 sentence frames**.
  - **Grammar:** present tense across persons, and common past-tense forms inside stories.
  - **Reading:** can read graded readers on her own.
  - **Speaking:** short, error-filled sentence conversations on familiar topics.
  - **What she will not have:** fluent conversation, native-speed cartoons, or accurate grammar across tenses.
- **Calibration warning.** Most per-hour figures come from teens and adults. For classroom-style, limited-input learning, younger starters learn **more slowly per hour** than older ones (Muñoz BAF project, below). Treat adult app numbers (Duolingo) as an **upper bound**.

## 3. Grammar and functions by stage (children)

- **Chunks first, then unpacked.** 16 child beginner classroom learners of French were followed for 2 years ([Myles, Hooper & Mitchell 1998/1999](https://www.essex.ac.uk/-/media/documents/departments/langling/myles-elct-15.pdf)).
  - Children start with whole memorized chunks (*comment t'appelles-tu*) and gradually break them into frames with slots.
  - When no chunk is available, their questions go from verbless → infinitive → finite verb.
  - This strongly supports the game's "frame plus slot" phrase design. **[CHILD] High.**
- **Cambridge YLE order** (English, but a good guide to pace for children):
  - Starters: present simple and continuous, *can*, *have got*, imperatives, *there is/are*, wh-questions.
  - **Movers adds the past simple**, comparatives and superlatives, *must*, *have to*, *would like* ([YLE handbook](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/network-services/yle-handbook-for-teachers.pdf); [Movers list](https://flyersenglish.com/grammar/movers)).
  - **Flyers adds the past continuous and present perfect** ([guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cambridge-yle-flyers/cefr-a2-grammar-structures/grammar-past-tenses-narrative)). Where *going to* and *will* first appear was **[unverified]**.
  - Medium confidence.
- **Spanish A1 → A2** (Plan Curricular and university A2 syllabi, adult):
  - A1: present indicative, regular plus key irregular verbs (tener, hacer, estar, ser, ir), *gustar*-type verbs, basic *ser/estar*.
  - A2: **pretérito indefinido and perfecto** (forms and contrast), with the **imperfecto** as a separate unit ([PCIC grammar index](https://cvc.cervantes.es/Ensenanza/biblioteca_ele/plan_curricular/indice.htm); [UAM A2 syllabus](https://www.uam.es/uam/media/doc/1607000283105/guia-docente-espanol-a2.pdf)).
  - I could not open the PCIC page to check exactly where *ir a + infinitivo* sits **[unverified]**. Medium confidence.
- **ser/estar.** VanPatten's sequence (1987), refined by [Perpiñán 2019](https://repositori.uic.es/handle/20.500.12328/2864?show=full):
  - Learners default to *ser*. **estar + adjective comes last** and stays overused for a long time ([Guijarro-Fuentes et al. 2023](https://docta.ucm.es/entities/publication/a88ea009-93c0-4891-9609-cf0a490f388a/full)).
  - Teach it as chunks (*¿Dónde está…?*, *está cansado*, *es grande*), not as a rule. **[ADULT] Medium.**
- **Past tense.** English speakers start with the preterite as the default past.
  - Dynamic and punctual verbs come first (*se cayó*, *comió*). The imperfect follows, through background description (*era*, *había*, *estaba*).
  - The Lexical Aspect Hypothesis behind this order is contested for Spanish ([Quintana Hernández 2020](https://ariasmontano.uhu.es/entities/publication/646136f9-a663-4df9-921e-d0ba30cae9e6); [Domínguez et al.](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/191281/1/Dominguez_Tracy-ventura_Arche_Mitchell_Myles_FINAL.pdf)). **[ADULT] Medium–Low.**
- **Comprehension runs ahead of production, even with huge input.** Canadian French immersion students reach near-native listening and reading, but their speech stays less accurate and less complex ([Kowal & Swain 1997 via CARLA](https://archive.carla.umn.edu/immersion/acie/vol1/May1998.pdf)).
  - Swain's *output hypothesis*: pushed speaking drives noticing of grammar.
  - US K-8 Spanish immersion: oral gains are steep early and **slow after program year 3** ([K-8 study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/287977595_Oral_Proficiency_Assessment_of_English-Proficient_K-8_Spanish_Immersion_Students)). **[CHILD] High.**
- **Comprehension-based teaching suits 6-year-olds.** With 6-year-old Japanese beginners, input-based tasks were as good as production-based ones, or better, for vocabulary and plural -s ([Shintani](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/incidental-acquisition-of-english-plural-s-by-japanese-children-in-comprehensionbased-and-productionbased-lessons/BFDF27042A1449A9197A70821B333927); [2016 book](https://linguistlist.org/issues/28/716/)). **[CHILD] Medium.**

## 4. Literacy, songs and stories, conversation

- **Reading.** Spanish spelling is shallow (regular). Most European children read accurately at the foundation level within their first school year, and English is **"more than twice as slow"** ([Seymour, Aro & Erskine 2003](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1348/000712603321661859)).
  - Letter-to-sound mapping **transfers** between English and Spanish ([Texas LD Center](https://texasldcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/10Key_ReadingBilingual_WEB.pdf)).
  - A child who already reads English should decode Spanish quickly. The new parts are the 5 pure vowels, ñ, ll/y, j/g, silent h, qu/gu and rr. **High** that it is cheap; **Low** that anyone has directly measured how fast English-reading children learn to decode Spanish.
  - Reading in the new language at primary age is feasible: an integrated spoken-plus-written course with 9–11-year-olds ran for 23 weeks ([Porter, Southampton](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/374731/1/A_20M_20Porter_20FINAL_20THESIS_20FRONT_2C_20LIT_20REVIEW_2C_20METH.pdf)). Evidence that it speeds vocabulary growth is thin. **Medium–Low.**
  - **Implication:** by Year 2, reading becomes the cheapest source of input and review.
- **Songs.** Small studies show gains, mostly receptive. Songs plus actions (TPR) beat either alone ([review](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/7ed63bc1-2e4b-4370-8d44-eab7a034cff9/full); [TPR+song](https://doaj.org/article/1508ea08bd03451395d807e228b44dca)). Songs also help motivation. **[CHILD] Medium–Low.**
- **Stories.** Repeated read-alouds **with brief explanation of new words** built children's vocabulary in Taiwan ([Lin 2014](https://nccur.lib.nccu.edu.tw/handle/140.119/49893)). Nine plain readings with no support produced **no significant word learning** ([Winn, JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/articles/26646-questioning-efficacy-reading-storybooks-very-young-japanese-efl-learners-vocabulary)). Stories need picture support, glossing and questions. **[CHILD] Medium.**
- **Conversation and interaction.** Interaction (negotiating meaning) helps children as well as adults, but few studies cover children learning a foreign language outside the country where it is spoken ([Philp, Oliver & Mackey 2008](https://lwc1.benjamins.com/catalog/lllt.23.toc); [EFL children](https://observatorio-investigacion.unavarra.es/documentos/5f7e68622999520bf9730ca7)).
  - Infants learned speech sounds from live tutors but **not from video or audio** ([Kuhl 2003](https://ilabs.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/kuhl_2003_pnas_1532872100.pdf)).
  - A conversational agent asking questions during story reading **matched a human partner on story comprehension** for 117 children aged 3–6, in their first language ([Xu et al.](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.7280/D10Q3P); [IBM summary](https://researcher.draco.res.ibm.com/publications/same-benefits-different-communication-patterns-comparing-childrens-reading-with-a-conversational-agent-vs-a-human-partner)).
  - Responsive in-game questioning is defensible; real human conversation is still the missing piece. **Medium.**
- **Speech recognition limits.** For non-native children's read speech, word error rate was **24.8%**, against 8% for native children (Whisper, Dutch) ([arXiv 2306.16710](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.16710)). Open-ended answers run about 33–36% ([arXiv 2306.04190](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.04190)).
  - Browser Web Speech es-MX is likely no better **[unverified]**.
  - Use the mic for "attempted and roughly right", as the game already does. Never use it to grade pronunciation or grammar. **Medium.**

## 5. Retention and attrition over breaks

- **Long-term memory for school Spanish** (733 people, 50 years; [Bahrick 1984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6242406/)):
  - Steep exponential loss for **3–6 years**, then stable for up to 30 years ("permastore").
  - How much survives depends on **how well it was originally learned**. **[ADULT] High.**
- **Spaced maintenance** ([Bahrick et al. 1993](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1993-bahrick.pdf)): **13 reviews at 56-day gaps ≈ 26 reviews at 14-day gaps** for 5-year retention. Long-gap maintenance is cheap. **[ADULT] Medium** (n=4; see [Cepeda 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)).
- **Children may lose words faster:**
  - Children aged 5–8 showed **word loss after 6 months** without input; 10–14-year-olds did not (Olshtain 1989).
  - In Cohen 1989, the younger child (9 vs 13) lost more after 9 months ([SSLA](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/attrition-in-the-productive-lexicon-of-two-portuguese-third-language-speakers/77CD5334E8540E6962CC6AA172118768)).
  - Some studies find the reverse ([review](https://repositorium.uminho.pt/bitstream/1822/11252/1/The%20effect%20of%20age_vers%c3%a3o%20publicada.pdf)). **[CHILD] Medium–Low** (small samples).
- **What goes first.** Productive vocabulary declines more than receptive after 3 months. Verbs and adjectives fade more than nouns. Practising production improves long-term retention (college Spanish, 18 months) ([Bardovi-Harlig 2020 review](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1269456.pdf)). **[TEEN]/[ADULT] Medium.**
- **Summer breaks.** I found no study of children's foreign-language learning over summer. General summer loss is about 1 month of skill, often recovered within weeks ([ERC](https://www.erc.ie/?p=9147)). **Low** (as applied to us).
- **Implications:**
  - Expect a 2–4 week break to cost **productive** recall more than recognition.
  - Build a "welcome back" review mode, and treat 5 minutes a week as maintenance.
  - Overlearning core words in Year 1 protects them for years.

## 6. Apps compared

- **Duolingo, reading and listening** **[ADULT]**, company-affiliated authors:
  - 225 US adults reached **Intermediate Low reading** and **Novice High listening** ([Jiang et al. 2021, *Foreign Language Annals*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/flan.12600)).
  - That took an **average of about 112 h**, comparable to 4 university semesters for reading and listening ([TechLearning](https://www.techlearning.com/news/does-duolingo-work)).
- **Duolingo, all skills** (Spanish, n=48, [LLT 2024](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1152/)): about **27 h → significant gains on all receptive and productive measures**.
- **Duolingo, what predicts gains** (n=287 over 6 months, [Sudina & Plonsky 2024](https://benjamins.com/catalog/jsls.00021.plo)):
  - Total minutes predicted **written but not oral** gains.
  - **How often learners practised and how far they progressed** predicted gains better than minutes.
- **App vs classroom** (16 weeks, [González-Fernández & de la Viña 2025](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/491d1cc7-ee33-4f80-8463-017360aafd78)): the app was better for receptive grammar and vocabulary; **the classroom was better for listening**. **Medium.**
- **Duolingo ABC** teaches English literacy to native-speaking preschoolers, so it is not second-language learning. Its exploratory study: 2 months of use ≈ 2 months of kindergarten ([EDC report](https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/edc-report-on-duolingo-abc.pdf)).
- **Lingokids** has only company-sponsored studies, for example a 6-week vocabulary study with 4–6-year-olds in Mexico whose results are not public ([Lingokids research](https://lingokids.com/research)). **Low.**
- **Gap:** I found **no independent peer-reviewed trial of a children's language app** that measures speaking or long-term retention. App evidence is adult, receptive-skewed and mostly vendor-funded.

## 7. Adult vs child evidence

- **Child-specific and solid:**
  - Cambridge YLE word lists and pacing.
  - Outcomes of US school and immersion programs.
  - Chunk-then-unpack grammar (Myles).
  - Comprehension running ahead of production (immersion).
  - Age vs rate in classrooms (Muñoz).
  - Comprehension-based teaching suiting young beginners (Shintani).
- **Adult or teen evidence applied to her:** CEFR vocabulary sizes, words per hour, Bahrick retention, all Duolingo hours and outcomes, receptive/productive ratios, the ser/estar and past-tense sequences.
- **Thin everywhere:** songs, storybooks without support, summer loss in a foreign language, app efficacy for children, speech recognition for non-native children.
- **Net adjustment:** discount adult per-hour rates by about **30–50%** for a 7–9-year-old. Expect a faster rate in Year 3 than Year 1, as her working memory, reading and chunk store grow.

## 8. Recommended realistic targets per year

Assumptions:
- About 75–90 h of play per year.
- A "word" is a lemma (*manzana/manzanas* counts once). Phrases and frames are counted separately.
- "Productive" means she can say it from a picture or situation without options.
- Ranges run from floor to realistic; "stretch" assumes a parent or tutor adds about 10 min of real conversation a week.

| | **Year 1 (age 7–8, ~80 h)** | **Year 2 (age 8–9, ~160 h total)** | **Year 3 (age 9–10, ~240 h total)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Level** | Pre-A1 (Starters); **ACTFL Novice Mid** | A1 (Movers); **Novice Mid → High** | A1 solid / A2 in listening and reading (Flyers); **Novice High**, Intermediate Low in listening and reading at a stretch |
| **Receptive vocabulary** | **400–550** (Starters list ≈ 470–510) | **800–1,000** (Movers ≈ 820–930) | **1,200–1,500**, stretch 1,800 (Flyers ≈ 1,300–1,450) |
| **Productive vocabulary** | **200–300** | **400–550** | **600–900** |
| **Phrases and grammar** | **15–20 frames plus about 40 fixed expressions**: greetings and courtesy; *Me llamo / Quiero / Me gusta / Tengo / ¿Dónde está? / Hay / ¿Cuántos?*; tú commands (*dame, toca, mira, ven*); numbers 1–20; noticing gender, plural and colour/size agreement; *ser/estar* only as chunks; yes/no and either/or questions | **35–50 frames**: present tense of about 25–30 high-frequency verbs in yo/tú/él forms as frames; negation; wh-questions (*qué, dónde, quién, cuántos, cómo, por qué*); **ir a + infinitive**; *me/te/le gusta*; *mi/tu/su*; *más… que*; place prepositions; days, time and weather; **past as story chunks heard only** (*fue, comió, se cayó, vi*) | **60–80 frames**: present tense across persons (nosotros, ellos); **preterite of about 20 common verbs** (heard and understood well; says yo/él forms); **imperfect understood in stories** (*había una vez, era, estaba*); reflexive routine chunks (*me levanto*); *ser* vs *estar* with common adjectives (errors expected); *lo/la* understood |
| **Listening** | Single requests with 1–2 details; short, slow sentences on familiar topics with picture support; 30–60 s picture stories | Follows 2–3-step instructions; a 2–3 min illustrated story in known words; simple questions in context at near-normal speed | Short learner-directed stories and cartoons; questions about herself at normal speed; the gist of new stories built from known words |
| **Speaking** | Words and memorized phrases; answers with one word or chunk; says a frame with a swapped slot | Builds sentences by swapping 2 slots (*Quiero dos manzanas rojas*); a short scripted exchange (name, age, likes, wants, where) | Retells a 4–6-sentence picture story; asks and answers in a 2–3 min conversation on familiar topics, with errors (stretch: Intermediate Low with real conversation practice) |
| **Reading** | **Decodes any regular Spanish word aloud** (phonics is reachable in Year 1); reads single words and captions | Reads simple sentences and short illustrated texts of 50–150 words on her own | Graded readers of about 300–1,000 words; writes short sentences (Spanish spelling is regular) |
| **Confidence** | Medium | Medium | Medium–Low (depends on her keeping it up and on real conversation) |

**How these targets were derived:**
- Year 1: about 80 h × 4–6 words per hour (Milton's teen rates, discounted for a child, but with a high-retrieval app) ≈ 320–480 words. That matches the Starters list (≈ 466–510) at about 90–100 GLH.
- Year 3: 220–275 h sits between the A1 and A2 Cambridge GLH bands and around Novice High on the NSE chart. Delaware's school program expects Novice High at year 4 with 90 min a week; daily play earns about one year of acceleration over that.

## 9. What this means for the end goal and how much content is needed

- **Pace of new words.** Reaching about 500 lemmas in Year 1 is about **1.5–2 new words a day on average**. That is far below the current cap of 4–6 per session (rule R2 in LEARNING_RESEARCH.md); the real limit is review load.
  - Today's 180 words and 6 frames are about **⅓ of Year 1 vocabulary and about 15% of Year 1 frames**. With daily play, that content runs out after about **3–4 months**.
- **Review load is what limits Years 2–3:**
  - Year 1: 500 words × at least 10 meaningful encounters ≈ 5,000–7,500 retrievals a year, or about 20 a day. That fits in 15 minutes.
  - Year 3: about 1,200 mature words reviewed every ~45 days ≈ 25–30 a day, plus new words, makes **about 45–50 retrieval events in 15 minutes**.
  - That is only workable if review is **woven into whole sentences, stories and listen-and-do**, where one sentence checks 3–5 words at once, instead of word-by-word drills.
- **How the game would evolve** (supported by the evidence above):
  1. **Year 1:** nouns, adjectives and fixed chunks in a world to explore (what the game is now), plus **Spanish phonics** (reading).
  2. **Year 2:** verbs, sentence frames and questions. **Illustrated read-aloud stories with glossing and questions** become the main way to get input. *ir a* for plans; the past only heard in stories.
  3. **Year 3:** stories become narratives in the past (the child retells them), plus graded readers and longer talks with characters that respond to what she says.
  - **Throughout:** say-it-to-get-it speaking (output), varied native voices (to make up for apps' weak listening), parent conversation prompts (the one ingredient an app cannot provide), and a welcome-back mode for breaks.
- **Measure progress against outside yardsticks:**
  - A picture-based receptive check like Pic-lex ([Alexiou](https://www.new.enl.auth.gr/piclex/)) against the Cambridge Starters, Movers and Flyers word bands.
  - An ACTFL-style can-do checklist on the grown-ups' Learning page at the end of each year.
