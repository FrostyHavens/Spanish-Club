# How kids learn words: research notes for Spanish Club

This is a summary of what research says about learning vocabulary in a new language, turned into design rules for this game. The player is about 7, reads English well, knows almost no Spanish, and plays 15-30 minute sessions on an iPad a few times a week. The game has about 78 words, no translations, and meaning comes from pictures, context and actions. The child answers by tapping and can also say words into the mic.

**How to read this.** Each section has three parts. *What the research says* gives the findings and numbers. *Caveats* says who was studied and how sure we can be. *What this means for our game* gives the design rules. Confidence labels:

- **High**: many studies agree, including some with children or in real classrooms.
- **Medium**: solid evidence, but mostly from adults or the lab, or the exact number is a judgement call.
- **Low**: little evidence, or the rule is our best guess from nearby findings.

**The main caveat.** Most vocabulary research studies university students learning English (often Japanese learners) or adults learning word pairs in a lab. Studies of 6-8-year-olds learning a foreign language are fewer and smaller. Every child-specific finding below is marked **[children]**. When a number comes from adults, we say so and treat it as a starting point to tune in playtests, not as a fact about our player.

## Summary: the rules with numbers

| R10 | Feedback after a wrong answer | **immediately**: show the right picture, replay the audio, ask again **1-3 minutes later** | High |
| R11 | Order of retrieval types | hear → pick picture, then read → pick picture, then picture → pick word, then **say it from the picture** | Medium |
| R12 | Speaking | ask for a word aloud only after **1-2 correct receptive answers**; accept fuzzy matches; after **2 misses** play the model, praise the try and move on | Medium |

| R14 | Verbs and commands | every verb gets an **on-screen action the child triggers**, and for at least **1** encounter the child says the command and sees it happen | Medium-High |
| R15 | The learning must be the game | understanding the word is what **moves the game forward** (opens the door, makes Canelo jump), not a quiz before it | Medium-High |

The sections below explain each rule. Section 11 puts them together as a word-strength model (a modified Leitner system) the game could use.

## 1. How many new words at once: cognitive load and chunking

**What the research says**

- **There is no research-backed number for words per lesson.** Figures in teacher guides range from about 3-7 to 20. Wallace (1984) suggests 5-7 new words per lesson. A figure of about **3-5 new items per lesson for young beginners** is often credited to Nation, but we could only find it second-hand. Gianfranco Conti, who writes widely on language teaching, says he has never found where the popular "8-10 per hour" comes from.
- **Working memory is small at age 7, and that is a firm limit.** Adults hold about **4 chunks** at once (Cowan); children hold fewer. Children 4-15 improve steadily with age (Gathercole et al., 2004). Children under about 7 don't yet reliably rehearse words in their heads. **[children]**
- **Older children remember more because they hold more chunks, not bigger ones** (Gilchrist, Cowan & Naveh-Benjamin, with 7-year-olds). A fixed phrase like *¿cómo estás?* or *dame la pata* can take up one slot instead of two or three. **[children]**
- **The number of words in a set matters less than spacing.** Studying one big stack of flashcards beat four small stacks, because each card comes back after a longer gap (Kornell, 2009, adults). Nakata & Webb (2016) report that set size mattered little once spacing was equal. A small set still helps a child's working memory in the moment, but it doesn't make learning stronger by itself.
- **Too many words at once means shallow learning and fast forgetting** (Cameron, 2001, *Teaching Languages to Young Learners*).

**Caveats.** The numbers per lesson come from teaching experience, not experiments. The working-memory findings are solid **[children]**, but they don't convert directly into a word count.

**What this means for our game**

1. **3-5 new words per errand. In any single dialogue or question, at most 3 things the child doesn't know yet.** (Medium)
2. **4-6 new words per 20-minute session, and never more than 8.** At 4-6 per session and 3 sessions a week, 78 words take about 4-6 weeks to introduce. That leaves the rest of each session for review, which matters more. (Low-Medium)
3. **Count fixed phrases as one word** (*buenos días*, *¿cómo estás?*, *dame la pata*, *por favor*). Always teach them whole and never split them into parts. (Medium)
4. **The real limit is how many words still need review, not how many have been introduced.** Wait to introduce new words until the words already met have had their first spaced reviews (section 3). (Medium)

## 2. How many encounters it takes to learn a word, and what counts as a good one

**What the research says**

- **Incidental learning from meaning-focused input is slow.** A meta-analysis of 24 studies (2,771 learners) found learners picked up only **9-18% of target words** on immediate tests and **6-17%** on delayed tests (Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa, 2023).
- **Repetition helps, with a medium effect.** A meta-analysis of 26 studies found the correlation between number of encounters and learning was **r = .34**. The effect varied with **learner age, spacing, visual support, engagement, and range of repetition** (Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa, 2019). Later reviews read the age result as **older learners getting more out of repetition**. That suggests a 7-year-old may need *more* encounters than an adult, not fewer.
- **Thresholds people often cite:**
  - Native speakers reading *A Clockwork Orange* learned most made-up slang words they met **about 10 or more times** (Saragi, Nation & Meister, 1978).
  - Learning showed up after **3 or more** encounters and was much stronger after **10** (Webb, 2007, adults learning English).
  - Readers of a graded reader remembered the meaning of **no word met fewer than 8 times** three months later (Waring & Takaki, 2003). Meeting a word 8 times was necessary, not enough.
  - In a classroom study with 4th graders, words taught in **12 encounters** beat words taught in 4 on deeper measures (McKeown, Beck, Omanson & Pople, 1985). **[children, first language]**
- **Learning on purpose with retrieval needs fewer encounters.** Learners who **recalled a word 5 or 7 times in one session did better than those who recalled it 1 or 3 times**, at every delay (Nakata, 2017, adults). Per minute of practice, though, one retrieval was the most efficient. So spread retrievals across days instead of piling them into one session (section 3).
- **A good encounter beats more of the same encounter.** Gains are larger when contexts are informative (Webb, 2007). A vivid, informative context, an action, or the child having to make a decision all count for more than seeing the word again (the Involvement Load Hypothesis: *need*, *search*, *evaluation*; Laufer & Hulstijn, 2001; support is mixed). Hearing a story read 3 times taught 7-8-year-olds some words. A brief explanation of each word added much more (Elley, 1989), and so did explanations in a preschool English-learner study (Collins). **[children]**

**Caveats.** The thresholds come mostly from adults reading. Our encounters have pictures and require action, which should make each one count for more. Our player is younger, which may mean more encounters are needed. The two probably cancel out to some extent. Treat "about 10" as a working estimate to check with in-game data.

**What this means for our game**

1. **Each word needs 10 or more meaningful encounters before the game treats it as solid. At least 5 should be retrievals** (the child has to produce or pick the meaning), not just seeing or hearing it. (Medium)
2. **A meaningful encounter is one where the child has to do something with the word:** pick it, use it, follow it, or say it. Spanish text the child only scrolls past counts for very little. (Medium)
3. **Make the first encounter as informative as possible:** the object is visible, the character points to it or acts it out, the word is spoken, and there are no competing unknowns. Later encounters can use leaner contexts. (Medium)
4. **Show each word in 3 or more different places or situations** (see sections 5 and 8). (Medium)
5. **Log encounters and retrievals per word** (the save already counts `right` and `wrong`) so we can find words that fall short in playtests. (High value, no research needed)

## 3. Spaced repetition: when to bring words back

**What the research says**

- **Spacing is one of the most reliable findings in learning research.** In Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve (1885), most forgetting happens in the first day. Many reviews and meta-analyses show that spreading practice out beats massing it (Cepeda et al., 2006; for language learning, Kim & Webb, 2022, 37 experiments, medium-to-large effect with variation).
- **The best gap depends on how long you want to remember.** In a study of 1,350+ people, the best gap between study sessions was about **20-40% of the time until the test** for a 1-week test, falling to **5-10%** for a 1-year test. Gaps that are too long hurt less than gaps that are too short (Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler, 2008, adults). If the goal is to remember about a month later, the best gaps are **about 3-7 days**.
- **Spacing also works within a session.** Longer gaps inside one session (about 3 minutes versus 30 seconds between repeats) help word learning (Nakata & Webb, 2016, and others).
- **[children] Spacing helps young children.** Spacing out lessons improved 5-7-year-olds' generalisation of science concepts (Vlach & Sandhofer, 2012). Three-year-olds learned new category labels better when examples were separated by 30 seconds of play (Vlach, Sandhofer & Kornell, 2008).
- **[children] In real classrooms the difference between gap lengths was small.** Hong Kong primary pupils learning 20 English words with 1-day or 8-day gaps learned little overall in one study, with a slight edge for the 1-day gap (Rogers & Cheung, 2018). A replication with 66 pupils and 3 sessions found **large gains and no difference** between 1-day and 8-day gaps (Rogers & Cheung, 2021). Having several spaced sessions seems to matter more than tuning the exact gap.
- **Expanding gaps (1, 3, 7 days...) are not clearly better than equal gaps for language vocabulary.** Nakata (2015) found no clear advantage. His 2018 review found no L2 vocabulary study showing one. Expanding schedules are still practical, because they free up time for new words as the vocabulary grows.
- **Spacing long-term.** Over 9 years, **13 sessions 56 days apart** gave retention similar to **26 sessions 14 days apart** (Bahrick et al., 1993, 4 adults learning 300 words).
- **Practical systems.** Pimsleur (1967) used gaps of 5 s, 25 s, 2 min, 10 min, 1 h, 5 h, 1 day, 5 days, 25 days... (no stated rationale). The **Leitner box** system (1972) moves a card up one box when you get it right and back when you miss, and reviews higher boxes less often. Duolingo replaced Leitner with **half-life regression**, which estimates each word's memory half-life from the learner's history. Prediction error dropped by **over 45%**, and daily retention rose **9.5%** in an A/B test (Settles & Meeder, 2016).
- **Learners prefer cramming, even though it works worse.** Spacing was better for **90%** of learners, yet **72%** believed massing had worked better (Kornell, 2009). Cramming *feels* fluent. The design has to take care of spacing, because the player won't.

**Caveats.** The direction (space it out) is **High** confidence, including for children. The exact gaps are **Medium**. They come mostly from adult studies, and the classroom studies with children found the gap length mattered less than having multiple sessions at all.

**What this means for our game**

1. **Every word gets retrieved in 3 or more different sessions on 2 or more different days** (ideally 3) before the game counts it as solid. (High for the direction, Medium for the count)
2. **Within the introducing session:** first retrieval after **1-3 minutes, with 2-4 other interactions in between** (not straight away). Second retrieval **5-10+ minutes later**, ideally somewhere else on the map or in another errand. (Medium)
3. **Between sessions, use expanding gaps, but don't worry about exact timing.** Aim for about **1 day → 3 days → 1 week → 2 weeks → 1 month**. If you schedule by session instead of by calendar (simpler for a game played about 3 times a week), use **next session → +2 → +4 → +8 sessions**. A word that comes up a little late is fine. Gaps that are too long hurt less than gaps that are too short. (Medium)
4. **Bring reviews in through the world, not through a review screen.** A townsperson asks about an old word, a notebook page comes back, an animal shows up again (section 7). (Medium)
5. **Keep the schedule as data.** Store `due` and `level` per word now, so we can switch to something smarter like half-life regression later if we want. (Low effort, high value)

## 4. Retrieval practice, active recall versus recognition, and desirable difficulty

**What the research says**

- **Being tested strengthens memory more than studying again.** Adults learning 40 Swahili-English pairs remembered about **80%** a week later if they kept being tested, and about **33-36%** if the words they had recalled were dropped from testing (Karpicke & Roediger, 2008). Later work found part of this was a spacing difference (Soderstrom, Kerr & Bjork), but the testing effect itself holds up across many studies.
- **[children] The testing effect works in primary school children.** Karpicke, Blunt & Smith (2016) tested elementary pupils and found benefits across individual differences. Benefits were larger for students with lower working memory (Agarwal et al., 2017). Preschoolers showed it with **cued recall and immediate feedback**, not free recall.
- **Wrong guesses help if the answer follows.** Attempting an answer and failing, then seeing the answer, beat just reading it (Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009). This worked with **immediate feedback**. With **delayed feedback** the benefit mostly disappeared (Hays, Kornell & Bjork, 2012).
- **Generation effect.** Information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you only read (Slamecka & Graf, 1978).
- **Desirable difficulties.** Spacing, mixing items, testing, and making the learner produce the answer all make learning feel harder and work better in the long run, as long as the learner can still succeed (Bjork, 1994; Bjork & Bjork, 2011).
- **Active recall versus recognition.** Recognising a word (picking its meaning) and recalling it (producing it) are different kinds of knowledge. Recognition is always ahead of recall, and the gap is bigger for rarer words (Webb). Tasks build mainly the kind of knowledge they practise (Webb, 2005, 2009). The ladder goes: recognise the form → recognise the meaning → recall the meaning → recall the form (say or write it).
- **Pictures can make learners think they know more than they do.** Learners may put less effort into words shown with pictures because the picture makes the word feel easy (Carpenter & Olson, 2012, adults). Results on pictures are mixed. They help most when the learner still has to work to retrieve the word.

**Caveats.** The testing effect is **High** confidence, including for elementary children. Errorful guessing with **immediate** feedback is **Medium-High**. The recognition-to-recall ladder is **Medium**, mostly from adult studies.

**What this means for our game**

1. **Count retrievals, not exposures.** A word only moves up a level after a **correct first-try answer**. Showing the word or replaying its audio doesn't count. (High)
2. **One correct answer is weak evidence.** With **3 options, a random tap is right 1 time in 3**. Two right in a row by chance is 1 in 9; three is 1 in 27. **Turning gold after the first correct use** (current behaviour, `S.learn`) works as a celebration, but treat it as "met" and not "known". Require **3 or more correct first-try answers on 2 or more different days** before the word is solid. Consider giving **at most 1 star per word per day** (`wordStars` currently counts `right` regardless of when). (High for the logic, Medium for the numbers)
3. **Move each word up the difficulty ladder over its life** (one step per level is a good default):
   1. **Hear the word** → pick the picture (recognition, with audio support)
   2. **Read the word only** (`text: true`) → pick the picture
   3. **See the picture only** (`pic: true`) → pick the Spanish word
   4. **See the picture → say it** (mic; spoken recall)
   5. **Use it in a new context**, like a request or a command to Canelo (Medium)
4. **Let the child guess, but always give feedback right away.** When the answer is wrong: highlight the right picture, replay the word, keep it friendly with no penalty, and **ask again 1-3 minutes later**. Never leave a wrong guess unresolved until a later session. (High)
5. **Keep difficulty desirable, not discouraging.** Aim for about **70-85% first-try correct** across a session (our estimate; research gives no exact figure). If a word fails twice in a row, step it down one rung on the ladder instead of repeating the same hard format. (Low-Medium)
6. **Not every encounter of a word should have its picture next to it.** Once a word is at level 2 or higher, show it in dialogue without the picture beside it, so the child has to recall the meaning (blue → gold already partly does this). (Medium)

## 5. Working out meaning from context and pictures

**What the research says**

- **Comprehensible input** (Krashen, 1985): we acquire language by understanding messages slightly beyond our level (*i+1*). The idea is very influential, but "+1" was never defined precisely, and later research gives output, interaction and feedback bigger roles than Krashen did. Take the core idea (understand first) and leave out the strong claim that input is *the only* thing that matters.
- **How much you need to know to infer the rest.** For reading without help, **98%** of words known gives comfortable comprehension and **95%** is a minimum. At **80%** nobody in the study understood adequately (Hu & Nation, 2000; Laufer, 1989; Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe, 2011, found a gradual relationship with no sharp cutoff). These figures are about **text without pictures**. A picture, an action, or a character pointing does much of the work a known word would.
- **[children] Picking the right referent is not the same as remembering it.** Toddlers are good at **fast mapping**: they pick the unfamiliar object when they hear an unfamiliar word (mutual exclusivity). But 24-month-olds **retained almost none** of those words 5 minutes later. Retention appeared when the adult **named the object again right after the child's choice** (Horst & Samuelson, 2008), or when the **target was highlighted and the other options dimmed** (Axelsson, Churchley & Horst, 2012). This research is with toddlers, but it maps directly onto our "pick the picture" moments.
- **[children] Cross-situational learning.** Infants learned **6 word-object pairs in under 4 minutes** from scenes that were each ambiguous, by tracking which word goes with which object across scenes (Smith & Yu, 2008). Older children and adults do this too (Roembke et al., 2023 review). This is learning from many scenes, not from one.
- **[children] Children learn words from stories.** 7-8-year-olds picked up some word meanings from hearing a story 3 times. Short explanations of the target words added much more (Elley, 1989).

**Caveats.** The coverage figures come from adult reading (**Medium** for us). Fast mapping without later retention is a **High**-confidence finding in toddlers, and its application to a 7-year-old in a game is a reasonable extension (**Medium**).

**What this means for our game**

1. **At most 1 unknown word per dialogue line.** Every other word should be learned already, a name, plain grammar, or shown by a picture or action. Lines are 3-6 words, so the coverage of *text* is lower than 95%. The **picture and the action make up the gap**, and that is why each unlearned word must keep its picture next to it. (Medium)
2. **Use inference by elimination on purpose for introductions:** **1 new word + 2 already learned words** as options. The child can work out the new one ("it's not *perro*, not *gato*, so it must be the bird"), and that feels like solving a puzzle. (Medium)
3. **After any correct inference, confirm it straight away:** say the word again, make the chosen picture bounce or glow, and dim the others. Without this, a correct pick may not stick. (Medium-High)
4. **Use each word in 3 or more different scenes** (shop, park, Canelo, notebook), so the child can learn from what the scenes have in common and not from a single scene. (Medium)
5. **No line should depend on understanding two new things at once.** If a request needs a new noun *and* a new number, at least one of them must already be known or clearly shown. (Medium)

## 6. Actions, gestures and combining modalities (TPR, dual coding)

**What the research says**

- **[children] 8-year-olds learned English words better with pictures or gestures than with audio alone.** Children studied for **20 minutes a day on 5 days**. Words learned with **pictures** or by **performing gestures** were remembered better than words learned by listening only, at **2 and 6 months** later. The benefit of pictures and of gestures was about the same (Andrä, Mathias, Schwager, Macedonia & von Kriegstein, 2020, three experiments). This study is the closest match to our player.
- **Gestures help adults too.** Doing a gesture while learning a word improves recall compared with verbal learning alone (Macedonia & von Kriegstein, 2012 review; Mayer et al., 2015). For adults, gestures helped more than pictures.
- **Dual coding** (Paivio): a word stored both as language and as an image has two ways to be recalled. The **picture superiority effect** is well established for memory in general. For learning words in a new language the picture benefit is real but inconsistent (Carpenter & Olson, 2012; section 4).
- **Total Physical Response** (Asher, 1969): learners respond physically to commands. It is very popular with young learners, but most studies are small, short, and lack delayed tests or control groups. The general claim (embodiment helps) is better supported by the gesture research above than by TPR studies themselves.

**Caveats.** The finding that pictures and gestures beat audio alone for children is **High** (several experiments with children, 6-month follow-up). TPR studies specifically are **Low** quality. On an iPad, "actions" are taps, drags, and things that happen on screen, which is a weaker form of physical involvement than a real gesture.

**What this means for our game**

1. **The first encounter of every word has picture + spoken audio + written form.** Tapping replays the audio. Never introduce a word as text only. (High)
2. **Verbs and commands (*siéntate, ven, salta, gira, dame la pata*) get an action:** Canelo does the trick and the child's command (tap or voice) makes it happen. **At least 1 encounter per verb should be the child giving the command and seeing it happen.** (Medium-High)
3. **Invite real gestures now and then**, without requiring them: "¡Salta tú también!" with the character jumping. A grown-up can join in. Never make progress depend on the camera or on a gesture. (Low-Medium)
4. **Pictures carry meaning early, then fade.** Once a word reaches level 2, sometimes ask for it without its picture next to it, so the picture doesn't stand in for retrieval (section 4). (Medium)
5. **Animal sounds and other sounds** (*guau, miau, cuac*) give a third cue alongside picture and word. Keep them paired with their animals. (Low-Medium)

## 7. What makes a learning game work

**What the research says**

- **Digital games help vocabulary on average, but how they are designed matters most.**
  - In a meta-analysis of 26 studies, games versus other activities showed a **large** effect. Comparing the same game with and without a design feature showed a **medium** effect. When everyone played the same game, other factors made no significant difference (Tsai & Tsai, 2018).
  - Another meta-analysis found that **game design** made a significant difference to effects (adventure-style games did better than non-adventure games), and **learner age did not** (Chen, Tseng & Hsiao, 2018).
- **[children] Intrinsic integration.** In a maths game for 7-11-year-olds, the version where the maths *was* the combat mechanic produced **more learning** than a version with quizzes between levels. In free time, children chose to play it **7 times longer** (Habgood & Ainsworth, 2011). A pre-registered replication with 210 participants also found more learning in the integrated version (Cutting & Iacovides, 2022).
- **Curiosity helps memory.** Curiosity comes from an **information gap**: you know something is there but not what it is (Loewenstein; Kang et al., 2009). When people were curious, they remembered the answer better and also remembered **unrelated material shown at the same time** better, both immediately and a day later (Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath, 2014, adults).
- **Duolingo.** Its research reports that adults who finished beginner Spanish or French reached reading and listening levels comparable to **4 semesters of university study** (Jiang et al., 2021, 225 adults). Duolingo staff are co-authors and the study is open to selection bias. Its clearer lesson is engineering: **personalised spacing** (half-life regression, section 3) measurably improved retention and how often people came back.
- **Watch out for rewarding the wrong thing.** If rewards come from activity (taps, streaks) and not from successful retrieval, players learn to chase the reward. Cramming *feels* productive (Kornell, 2009).

**Caveats.** Game meta-analyses mix short, small studies with many designs (**Medium**). The finding that integrated learning gets played longer is **Medium-High** **[children]**. The curiosity findings come from adult lab studies (**Medium**).

**What this means for our game**

1. **Understanding the word must be how the game moves forward.** Canelo only sits if you say or pick *siéntate*. The baker only gives you the bread if you ask for *el pan*. Avoid quizzes placed in front of the fun as a gate. (Medium-High)
2. **Give rewards for retrieval, not exposure.** Stars, hearts, and album entries come from correct first-try answers and from speaking, spread over days. Avoid streaks that punish missed days. (Medium)
3. **Use information gaps:** hidden notebook pages, a picture bubble over someone's head, a locked barn, an animal you can hear but not see. Show that something is there before showing what it is. (Medium)
4. **Keep sessions at 15-30 minutes with a natural stopping point** (an errand done, the day ending). The 20-minute daily sessions in Andrä et al. (2020) worked for 8-year-olds. (Medium)
5. **Make reviews part of the world** (a townsperson's request, a returning animal, a page to complete), so that 40-60% of prompts can be reviews without feeling like drills. (Low-Medium)
6. **No time pressure** on answers. Speed comes later, and pressure works against the curiosity that drives play. (Low-Medium)

## 8. Which words in which order: frequency, concreteness and grouping

**What the research says**

- **Concrete words are easier to learn and to keep.** Concrete words and cognates were learned better than abstract words and non-cognates, and **forgotten more slowly**. The effect of word frequency was small (de Groot & Keijzer, 2000, "What is hard to learn is easy to forget", adults).
- **Frequency and usefulness.** Nation's general advice is to teach high-frequency, high-utility words first. For a 7-year-old's game world, "useful" means *useful in the game*: greetings, *sí/no*, *por favor*, and the objects of the errands.
- **Similar words learned together interfere with each other.** Learning a set of near neighbours together (all colours, all fruits) took **more trials** than learning unrelated words (Tinkham, 1993). Waring (1997) explained this by interference and recommended not presenting new words as sets under one category. **Thematic** groups (words that belong to one scene: *frog, pond, swim, green*) helped or did no harm (Tinkham, 1997). **Later evidence is mixed**: some studies found no difference between semantic and thematic grouping (Hippner-Page, 2000; Liu, 2003), and Nakata & Suzuki (2019) found spacing changes the picture.

**Caveats.** The concreteness result is **High** (many studies, though mostly adults). The interference result is **Medium**: it is consistent in early lab studies with made-up words, and mixed later. Our current notebook pages are organised by category (*Los colores, Más números, La granja*), which is fine for a **reference notebook** but is exactly the grouping the research cautions against for **introducing** new words.

**What this means for our game**

1. **Order of introduction:** greetings and *sí/no/por favor* as fixed phrases → **concrete, picturable nouns** (animals, food, places) → **action verbs and commands** (show them, section 6) → **adjectives** like colours and feelings, attached to nouns the child already knows (*la pelota roja*) → **abstract or emotional words** last, in rich contexts (a sad face, a tired character yawning). (Medium-High)
2. **Introduce at most 2 new words from the same tight set per errand** (colours, numbers, farm animals, fruits). Spread the rest across errands, e.g. a picnic errand: *leche, queso* + *tres* + *rojo*. (Medium)
3. **Group by situation, not by category, when introducing words.** An errand is a theme: everything in the bakery errand belongs to the bakery scene. Notebook pages can stay organised by category, because they serve as review and reference. (Medium)
4. **Bring similar words together on purpose only after each is at level 2 or higher.** Telling *rojo* from *rosa*, or *seis* from *siete*, is then useful discrimination practice and not interference. (Medium)
5. **Look for cognates and use them early** (e.g. *el elefante*, *el chocolate*, *la música*, *el tigre*, if new words are added): they are easier and stick longer. Never put a cognate up against a non-cognate distractor that looks similar. (Low-Medium)
6. **Numbers 1-10 are a special case.** Children learn them as a counting sequence, and counting itself is good practice. Still, introduce *seis-diez* over 2 or more errands, not all 5 at once as *Más números* does now. (Low)

## 9. Speaking: speech recognition, feedback and anxiety

**What the research says**

- **Speech recognition is much less accurate for children, especially young ones.** Reported word error rates: about **5%** for adults on commercial systems against **15-21% for ages 6-10** (The Learning Agency's industry review; not peer-reviewed). Age is the biggest factor (2025 analysis of errors). Recognition also drops with background noise and spontaneous speech. Our setup recognises single words against a short list of options, which is far easier than open transcription, but there will still be false "misses".
- **[children] Pronunciation training with speech recognition can work.** 11-year-olds learning English pronunciation improved about as much with a speech-recognition tutor as with a teacher, on isolated words, in the short term (Neri, Mich, Gerosa & Giuliani, 2008). Corrective feedback from automated systems helped adults learning Dutch (Neri, Cucchiarini & Strik, 2006).
- **Saying a word is retrieval in its hardest form** (recalling the spoken form, section 4). Repeating right after hearing it trains pronunciation. **Saying it from a picture, without hearing it first,** is the retrieval practice that builds vocabulary.
- **Anxiety.** Anxiety in language learners is mostly triggered by **speaking on the spot** and by being evaluated (Horwitz's scale and studies adapted for adolescents). We found almost no research on 7-year-olds specifically, so this part is extrapolation.
- **Our own playtest:** the child's first wish for more was *"not many options for using the microphone"*. Speaking is motivating for this player.

**Caveats.** Speech recognition accuracy numbers vary a lot by system and test (**Medium**). The anxiety evidence comes from older learners (**Low** for our player).

**What this means for our game**

1. **Ask for a word aloud only after 1-2 correct answers recognising it.** Before that, the mic offer is "repeat after me", which practises pronunciation and does not count as retrieval. (Medium)
2. **Move from repeating to recalling:** at first the word is spoken and the child says it back. Later the child sees the picture and says it **before** hearing it. Only spoken recall counts as a level-4 retrieval. (Medium)
3. **Be generous with the recognition result, never punishing.** Accept fuzzy matches, other forms (*perrito*), with or without the article. **After 2 misses**, play the model word, praise the attempt ("¡Casi! Escucha: *perro*"), and move on. **Never block progress** on the mic. A recogniser miss must never look like the child got it wrong. (High)
4. **Never put similar-sounding words** (*ven/bien*, *dos/tres* is fine) **in the same mic question** (already in CONTENT.md). (High)
5. **More speaking, built into play:** 2-3 moments per errand where speaking is the natural action (commanding Canelo, greeting townsfolk, ordering at the bakery), plus the optional mic on every word question. Speaking stars go toward the word's strength. (Medium)
6. **Keep it private and low-stakes:** no scores on speaking, no "wrong" sounds, and the mic stays a choice. A grown-up switch is fine. (Low-Medium)

## 10. Inspiration from games: Tunic, Chants of Sennaar, Heaven's Vault

**What the designers did**

- **Tunic** (Andrew Shouldice, 2022). Shouldice says the game came from his memory of being a young child **who couldn't yet read** the words in games, and of poring over NES manuals full of secrets. The in-game manual is written mostly in an invented script, with **a few English words as anchors**. Its pages are **found gradually** around the world, and the **illustrations, maps and arrows carry the meaning**. Pages you couldn't understand early on become understandable later. The aim was to make the player feel "a stranger in a strange land" in a world "bursting with secrets".
- **Chants of Sennaar** (Rundisc, 2023). Each culture's language was built "for gameplay, not linguistics", with a small vocabulary (**about 30-40 words per language**, by one secondhand account). The player writes their own guesses in a journal. As players commonly describe it, a **journal page of 3-5 drawings is confirmed only when all of its words are matched correctly**, so random guessing doesn't work and the player has to reason. The glyphs share visual patterns, so related words look related.
- **Heaven's Vault** (inkle, 2019). The player chooses a translation from a few candidates and **gets no immediate feedback**. Confidence comes from seeing the same word in **many different inscriptions**. Players found their own strategies, such as "long glyph = complex meaning".

**What learning research adds.** Tunic's partly readable pages and Sennaar's confirmation by page line up well with comprehensible input, cross-situational learning and retrieval practice. Heaven's Vault's **lack of feedback** goes against the evidence for young learners (immediate feedback was what made errorful learning work, section 4). A 7-year-old needs quick confirmation.

**What this means for our game**

1. **The notebook should gradually become readable.** Old pages should get easier as words turn gold, and the game should give reasons to **revisit old pages** (a sticker to complete, a secret only noticeable later). Each revisit is a free spaced review. (Medium)
2. **Sennaar-style page puzzles as reviews.** Match **4 pictures to 4 Spanish words** on a notebook page, and the page is confirmed only when all 4 are right. Random tapping succeeds **1 time in 24**, so success is real evidence of knowing the words. Use only words at level 2 or higher. (Medium)
3. **Keep immediate feedback.** Unlike Heaven's Vault, confirm or correct each answer within a second or two. A hunch can still be allowed ("I think *pato* is the duck"), but confirm it the next time the word appears, within minutes and not sessions. (High for children)
4. **Learned words act as Tunic's English anchors:** gold words make new text easier to read. Write lines so new words appear **next to gold words** (section 5). (Medium)
5. **Leave things to wonder about:** a corner of the map, a page, or an animal the child can't fully understand yet, so there is a reason to come back. (Low-Medium; this is design taste, not data)

## 11. Putting it together: a word-strength model

A modified Leitner system that follows the rules above. It stores 2 numbers per word (`level`, `due`) next to the existing `right`, `wrong` and `said`.

| Level | Meaning | How it is asked | Next due after a correct first try |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Seen**: met in a sentence or on a page | picture + audio + word (exposure) | ask in the same scene, **after 1-3 minutes** |
| 1 | **Met**: 1 correct answer (gold card) | hear → pick picture (3 options, 1 new + 2 known) | **5-10+ minutes later**, same session |
| 2 | **Recognised** | read only → pick picture | **next session** (at least 1 day) |
| 3 | **Known** | picture only → pick word; notebook match puzzles | **+2-3 days** (or 2 sessions) |
| 4 | **Spoken** | picture → say it (mic), or a command in a new context | **+1 week** (or 4 sessions) |
| 5 | **Solid** | any format, mixed with similar words | **+2-4 weeks** (or 8 sessions), then occasionally |

- **When the child misses:** give immediate feedback, ask again in **1-3 minutes**, and drop **1 level** (not back to 0).
- **Solid** requires **3 or more correct first-try answers on 2 or more different days**, including 1 or more at level 3 or higher. On average that means about **10-15 encounters** per word (R5, R6).
- **Each session:** pick due words first, oldest overdue first, up to about **40-60%** of prompts. Then introduce **4-6 new words** if fewer than about **10 words** are still in levels 0-1. Otherwise, introduce none.
- **Mic off:** level 4 can be reached through text-only recall (picture → pick from 4 written words) instead of speaking.
- **Check in playtests:** first-try accuracy per level (target about **70-85%**), words that stay stuck at level 1-2, and how many due reviews actually come up in a session.

These numbers are a **starting point** pieced together from research mostly done with adults. Tune them with your own child's data.
